Games that started as Half-Life 2 mods

FeatureSource engineUpdated 2026

Plenty of games have had good mod scenes. Half-Life 2's did something rarer: it produced games that created genres that didn't exist before, and which other studios then spent a decade making.

Four of them, and what each one turned into.

Garry's Mod

Released as a simple Half-Life 2 mod in December 2004 — the same month HL2 itself was still new — and given a retail release in 2006.

It wasn't a game. It was a physics sandbox: spawn things, weld them together, see what happens. That's the entire pitch, and it turned out to be enough, because Garry's Mod became a platform rather than a product. Whole game modes were built inside it, several of which grew larger audiences than most commercial releases.

It's the clearest demonstration of what Source made possible. The physics system Valve built to make crates behave convincingly turned out, when handed to players with no objective attached, to be a toy with no bottom.

The Stanley Parable

A Half-Life 2 mod that used the engine to tell a story about being told a story — a narrator describes what you're doing, and the game becomes an argument between you and him about whether you'll do it.

It found more than 90,000 downloads in its first two weeks, which for a mod with no marketing meant it had struck something. A standalone remake followed two years later and repeated the success.

What makes it a Source story specifically: the mod needed almost nothing from the engine except corridors, doors and a voice. It's the counter-example to the assumption that a powerful engine is for powerful spectacle. Half-Life 2's toolset was good enough that someone could ignore all of it and still ship.

Dear Esther

Emerged from its Half-Life 2 mod origins as a standalone game in 2012 — a windswept Hebridean island, a fragmentary voiceover, no enemies, no puzzles, no fail state.

It's the origin point of an entire genre. The wave of narrative-exploration games that followed — Gone Home, Firewatch, and everything the internet insisted on calling walking simulators — traces back to Dear Esther proving that an audience existed for a first-person game about nothing but moving and listening.

That it was built in the engine behind one of the most kinetic shooters ever made is the joke that keeps giving.

Black Mesa

The most ambitious of the four: rebuild the whole of Half-Life 1 inside Source.

A volunteer team of around forty people took on a project most studios would have priced in years and millions, and Valve eventually granted permission for a standalone commercial release — an unusually generous outcome, and one that says as much about Valve's attitude to its modding community as any SDK release.

Worth being precise: Black Mesa is a remake of Half-Life 1 built as a Half-Life 2 mod. It's not a Half-Life 2 story, and it's not a GoldSrc mod — it's HL1's content rebuilt on HL2's engine, which is exactly why it needed to be a total conversion rather than a patch.

Why Half-Life 2 specifically?

Three things lined up that rarely line up together:

FactorWhy it mattered
The SDK was free Valve released real development tools at no cost. No licence negotiation, no budget — the barrier was time, not money.
The engine solved the hard parts Physics, animation, facial expression and AI were done. A two-person team could work on their idea instead of on rendering.
Steam existed A finished mod could reach millions without a publisher. Before Steam, "it got popular" and "it made money" were unrelated events.

Take any one away and the list above gets much shorter. Free tools with no distribution produce hobby projects nobody sees; distribution without tools produces nothing at all.

Could it happen again?

The path is different now rather than closed. Engines like Unity and Unreal are free to start with, so an ambitious developer no longer needs someone else's game as a foundation — the reason to mod Half-Life 2 in 2005 was that it was the cheapest way to get a working engine, and that reason is gone.

What Half-Life 2 still offers is the thing that never got cheaper: a world already built. City 17 exists, the Combine exist, the physics feel right. That's why the Workshop added in the 2024 anniversary update matters — it lowered the distribution barrier for HL2 content to a single click, twenty years after the games above had to clear it the hard way.

Frequently asked

Do I need Half-Life 2 to play these?

Not for the standalone versions — all four are sold as their own products. Owning Half-Life 2 does give Garry's Mod access to its assets, which a lot of community content assumes you have.

Are the original mod versions still available?

Sometimes, through community archives, and they're genuinely interesting to compare against the commercial releases. The standalone versions are what to play if you just want the game.

Is Portal a Half-Life 2 mod?

No — though it's a related story. Portal grew out of a student project that Valve hired the team behind, rather than out of the HL2 mod scene. Different route to the same engine.

What about Team Fortress?

Older, and from the other game — Team Fortress began as a Quake mod, and Team Fortress Classic was its Half-Life 1 version. It predates the Half-Life 2 scene entirely.
